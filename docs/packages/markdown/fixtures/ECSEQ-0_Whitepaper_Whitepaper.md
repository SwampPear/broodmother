# Abstract

A dense array of microelectrodes that measures its own interfacial electrochemistry, read out by a model trained to interpret that measurement, is a general-purpose biological instrument. The array is passive and largely fixed; the discrimination lives in the decoder, so the same hardware serves different assays by retraining the model rather than refabricating the chip, and it improves as models improve. This paper characterizes such an instrument: a high-density passive array performing localized electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) at each pixel, with a per-pixel geometry (pitch, active-site size) set to match the target analyte rather than fixed by the architecture. We develop it against its first and most demanding application, label-free and wash-free DNA sequencing by synthesis (SBS), because nucleotide incorporation is a fast, weak, four-way discrimination that stresses the signal chain harder than the surface-binding assays the same array also supports. During polymerase-mediated synthesis the interfacial impedance is perturbed in a base-dependent manner; measured directly by EIS — no label to image, no reagent to exchange between incorporations — that perturbation separates into three frequency-distinct axes (kinetic, molecular, faradaic) carrying enough information to discriminate all four bases, which a two-stage decoder — a per-event spectral encoder followed by a temporal transformer — reads. We report two pre-hardware checkpoints on physically simulated data. At a short read ($L=30$), the regime the interfacial screening geometry actually permits ([[Appendix]] §E), the decoder reaches 71.2% per-base (Phred Q5.4) against a Q20 (99%) production floor; at a longer read ($L=100$), beyond the array's supported sensing horizon and therefore a statement about decoder capacity rather than an operating point, temporal context lifts accuracy to 89.6% per-base and the faradaic axis lifts the per-event encoder from 54.5% to 76.7%. These numbers characterize the information content of the simulated signal and the decoder that inverts it, not a physical device, which does not yet exist. Because the readout is electrical and continuous rather than optical and cyclic, the throughput bottleneck sits on compute and array area rather than on fluidics, and the architecture-independent figure of merit is the areal base rate $\eta P / t_\text{base}$ — about $2.1\times10^{6}$ bases/s per mm² of active area — which depends only on pixel count and polymerase turnover. Under it a single-run human genome is a parallelism condition, $\eta P L \ge B$: an array-area claim reachable at a stated scale (~2,200 mm² at 0.5 µm pitch) and gated on Q20 per-base accuracy. That endpoint, and the population-scale sequence a general genotype-to-phenotype model would ultimately consume, are the aspiration the platform points toward rather than a claim it makes for the instrument in hand; the near-term device is a short-read, application-agnostic electrochemical sensor of which targeted DNA panels are the first buildable case.

# 1. Introduction

Suppose an event at an electrode leaves an electrical trace. A base added to a strand of DNA, a target binding to a probe, a molecule giving up an electron: each one moves charge, and moving charge changes how the interface looks to a small AC signal. If those changes are distinct enough to tell apart, then an array of electrodes plus a model trained to read them is a measuring instrument, and an unusual one. The electrode interprets nothing. It reports the state of its own surface. Whatever meaning that report carries is supplied afterward by the decoder, so the same chip can be pointed at a different question by retraining rather than refabricating, and it improves as models improve rather than as fabrication improves. Pitch and active-site size are chosen to match whatever is being measured; they are properties of the assay, not of the platform. This paper is about that instrument, developed against the hardest task we could give it.

We chose DNA sequencing as that task because the signal is weak, the event is fast, and the answer has to be one of four. If the architecture holds there, easier measurements follow. The reason to care is not the price of a genome, which has already fallen from roughly three billion dollars to a few hundred [76,4,5]. It is that most of the medicine waiting on sequence is waiting for it somewhere inconvenient. A model that learns how genotype produces phenotype needs sequence from millions of people, not thousands [6,7]. A clinician with a critically ill newborn needs an answer within the shift, not the week [8]. A diagnostic that belongs in a doctor's office needs an instrument that is small, cheap, and free of fluidics to maintain. None of these asks for a whole genome first. Each asks for a short, targeted read, produced cheaply, near the person who needs it.

Today's high-throughput platforms read a base one of two ways. They image a fluorescent label attached during each cycle, or they detect a chemical byproduct released when the base goes in [3,9]. Both work well, and both pay for it in the same currency. An optical platform needs something to attach a label to, an exposure to capture it, and a wash to clear the previous cycle before the next can start. The wash and the exposure set a floor on cycle time, demand fluidics precise enough to dominate the cost of the machine [4], and consume a flow cell that is discarded at the end of every run. Bulk electronic platforms drop the optics but still reset between cycles, and still read a chemical proxy rather than the incorporation itself [9]. In both cases the instrument spends most of its time on housekeeping: moving liquid, resetting the reaction, and taking a picture between every base of a process that is underneath continuous.

ECSEQ does not label the base. It reads the electrical disturbance the incorporation itself makes. While a polymerase extends a surface-bound strand, the impedance of the electrode-electrolyte interface shifts, and the shift depends on which base went in. Measuring that shift by EIS at each pixel is the whole measurement. Nothing is labeled, nothing is imaged, and no reagent is exchanged between incorporations, because all four nucleotides are present throughout. What was a fluidics problem becomes a readout problem: how many pixels can be sampled, how fast, and how much compute the basecalling costs. That is a more tractable problem, because it is the kind that ordinary circuit engineering and falling compute prices keep solving.

Reading the event electrically also changes what else the chip can do. The array is passive and the discrimination lives in the model, so one array and one reader cover many assays with a retrained decoder. Sequencing is the first and hardest of them, not the whole of them, and it is the one that pays a specific price. The incorporation signal is visible only while the growing strand remains within a few nanometers of the electrode, which bounds reads to tens of bases ([[Appendix]] §E). Sequencing therefore buys parallelism with read length, which is a fair trade, since time to a finished genome depends on read length only through how many runs it takes. Measurements of things that stay at the surface — a hybridized target, an antibody-antigen pair, a redox-active metabolite — never reach that limit at all.

Three things had to be true for this to be worth attempting now, and none of them held for earlier electrochemical sequencing work. Thin-film electrode arrays can be patterned densely enough to give each clonal cluster its own sensor at micron pitch. Polymerase kinetics are understood quantitatively enough to connect an incorporation event to an expected electrical signature [10,11]. And models are now good enough to recover base identity from a noisy multi-dimensional spectrum by using the sequence around it. Each is unremarkable alone. Together they make per-event impedance sequencing worth testing.

Reading incorporation this way raises three questions, and they set the order of the paper. Is the signal actually base-specific and repeatable? We argue that it separates into three frequency bands with different physical origins: a kinetic band from how long the polymerase dwells, a molecular band from the nucleotide's dielectric coupling to the gold, and a faradaic band from base-dependent oxidation, which is what separates A from G. Together they carry enough information for all four bases. Is a single event strong enough to call? On its own, no, so each landed template is grown into a clonal cluster and read as an ensemble average, and the decoder reads a whole read in context rather than one event in isolation. Can the readout scale without an amplifier under every pixel, which would give back the cost advantage of a passive array? We treat that as a data-acquisition problem and work it through in the appendix.

The contributions are: (i) a passive electrochemical sensor architecture in which the same metal film anchors the DNA and transduces the incorporation signal; (ii) a physical account of the base-specific impedance signature and the three frequency-separated axes it decomposes into; (iii) a two-stage basecaller, a per-event spectral encoder followed by a temporal transformer that reads the cluster in context; (iv) an evaluation on physically simulated reads, with a throughput and read-length analysis that states the conditions under which the architecture reaches whole-genome scale; and (v) a front-end data-rate and basecalling-compute budget, together with an end-to-end simulation of the readout signal chain that locates its binding constraint at the analog front end ([[Appendix]] §D). We keep read length and throughput apart throughout, because they are separate constraints: read length is bounded by interfacial screening geometry at the sensor, throughput by parallelism in the readout. Neither limits the other, and conflating them makes one read-length figure carry two unrelated claims. One caveat governs everything that follows. No chip exists yet. Every number reported here comes from a physics simulator, and fabricating the first pixel is our immediate concern after filing this whitepaper and the associated patents.

# 2. Related Work

ECSEQ sits at the intersection of three lines of work: label-free electronic sequencing, the readout of polymerase kinetics during synthesis, and electrochemical impedance sensing of DNA.

## Label-free and electronic sequencing

The closest commercial precedent is semiconductor sequencing, in which an array of ion-sensitive field-effect transistors detects the proton released upon nucleotide incorporation, achieving non-optical, natural-base sequencing at scale [9]. That work establishes label-free electronic sequencing by synthesis as a viable modality. It measures a bulk pH change integrated over each well, however, rather than a per-event impedance signature, and does not resolve the incorporation event itself.

A closer architectural precedent is molecular-electronics sensing, in which a passive semiconductor chip records real-time single-molecule enzyme activity electrically at chip scale, including polymerase-class binding kinetics [73]. This is the nearest reported analogue to the architecture described here, namely a passive electrode array transducing an enzymatic event as it occurs. It differs in the quantity measured, resolving binding kinetics at individual molecules rather than an ensemble incorporation signature decoded into base identity.

At the single-molecule limit, recognition tunneling through a functionalized nanogap established per-base electronic discrimination [74,75], and graphene field-effect transistors have subsequently been shown to discriminate individual nucleobases electronically [12]. These constitute the closest precedent for molecule-specific electronic readout, but operate on static molecules rather than during polymerase-mediated incorporation. ECSEQ differs from both in transducing a per-event, per-pixel impedance perturbation generated during active synthesis.

## Polymerase-kinetics readout

Single-molecule real-time sequencing established that polymerase dynamics are directly readable during synthesis, calling bases from the timing of fluorescent pulses emitted as the enzyme incorporates them [10]; subsequent work showed that base modifications can be inferred from incorporation kinetics alone [13]. These results ground the premise underlying the kinetic axis of the present architecture, namely that base-dependent polymerase dwell carries callable information. They also establish a constraint on its use: the kinetic signal is weak at the level of a single event and requires aggregation over a clonal ensemble before it becomes separable. ECSEQ transduces the same underlying kinetics through their effect on interfacial impedance rather than through an optical pulse.

## Electrochemical impedance sensing of DNA

Electrochemical impedance sensing is an established, analyte-general biosensor modality on functionalized microelectrode arrays, applied label-free to proteins, whole cells, and pathogens as well as to nucleic acids [77]. The application-generality claimed for the present platform (§1, §7) derives from this array-based biosensing lineage rather than from a sequencing-specific one, with sequencing representing its most demanding member rather than a distinct technology. Within this lineage, impedance-based DNA sensing is well characterized for hybridization, where EIS detects the formation or mismatch of a pre-formed duplex on a functionalized electrode [14,15]. This body of work grounds the interfacial chemistry and the measurement modality on which ECSEQ builds, but it interrogates static duplexes rather than active synthesis. To our knowledge, no prior work demonstrates real-time base discrimination during synthesis by impedance, which is the capability targeted here.

# 3. Overview

ECSEQ centers on a high-density passive sensor array that performs localized electrochemical impedance spectroscopy at each pixel, and on the model that decodes the per-pixel signal — a model retrained per assay rather than a fixed pipeline. The signal chain below is traced for the sequencing case, the most demanding one; a surface-binding assay uses the same array and reader with a different decoder and a pitch chosen for its analyte. It runs end to end as follows. A clonal cluster of identical template copies is grown over each pixel, lifting the per-event signal from a single molecule to a measurable ensemble average. During synthesis, all four natural nucleotides are present in a single pool, without wells, and a polymerase extends each surface-bound primer processively. As each base is incorporated, an EIS sweep at that pixel captures the base-dependent perturbation of the interfacial impedance. The per-event spectrum is passed to a downstream basecaller. A per-cycle encoder compresses each spectrum into an embedding, and a temporal transformer reads the whole cluster's embedding sequence to call each base in sequential context. What one pixel does — grow a cluster, read an incorporation, call a base — is the whole of the sequencing architecture; the platform claim is that same operation replicated across the array. For sequencing the array is operated along a continuum from targeted panels, buildable now, to whole-genome scale, gated on the short-read physics of §7 (the operating points are laid out in §4); beyond sequencing the same array reads surface-bound analytes the read-length limit never reaches, with the decoder the only part that changes.

The sections that follow trace this chain in order. We first describe the sensor stack, the surface chemistry that makes an event measurable, the physical origin of the base-specific impedance signature, and the three axes along which it separates the bases (§4), and then the model that decodes the signal (§5). Because the platform is pre-hardware, the model is currently trained on reads from a physics-based simulator. We describe both the training procedure and the planned inversion to real data once a chip is available. §6 reports basecalling accuracy on simulated reads, and §7 discusses limitations and the path to hardware.

# 4. Methods: Sensor Architecture and Signal

Sections A–D describe the instrument itself — the electrode stack, its surface chemistry, and the interfacial-impedance signal it transduces along three discrimination axes. These are application-general: the same array and the same three-axis physics read any surface electrochemistry a decoder is trained to interpret, with pitch and active-site size chosen to match the analyte. Section E is the sequencing-specific layer — the loop that turns this instrument into a basecaller. We describe the general parts first.

## A. Sensor Stack

Every pixel is a vertical stack of a top gold (Au) electrode and an underlying platinum (Pt) electrode separated by an anodized Al₂O₃ dielectric. The Au layer is the chemical interface that binds the DNA template, and the Pt layer is the impedance transducer. The array is addressed passively over row and column interconnects, with a polymer fill isolating adjacent pixels to confine signal and suppress crosstalk (Fig. 2). Localized per-pixel EIS is the measurement primitive throughout [16], and the Pt interface provides a stable, low-noise double-layer response across the full frequency sweep [17]. During operation, an EIS sweep at each pixel is synchronized to polymerase-mediated incorporation, and the resulting perturbation in interfacial impedance is the raw per-event signal passed to the basecaller. The off-chip readout that services the passive array — excitation, analog front-end, code-division multiplex, and digital demux — is analyzed end to end as a five-stage chain in [[Appendix]] §D.8.

![[whitepaper-basic-stack.png]]

**Device scales.** The architecture is pitch-invariant, and we refer to three distinct device scales throughout. ECSEQ denotes the platform and the sensing architecture; ECSEQ-1 denotes the first physical device built on it. The ECSEQ-1 prototype is a 4-pixel, direct-wired die whose sole purpose is to test whether a real incorporation produces a base-discriminating impedance signature; it performs no sequencing. The ECSEQ-1 full-spec die is a 960 × 960 array (921,600 pixels, 3.69 mm² active area) — a discrimination demonstrator and targeted-panel device, not a genome sequencer. Panel scale refers to a ~10⁸-pixel array (~900 mm²), the regime in which a single-run human genome becomes possible. Sections 4 through 6 concern the architecture and are scale-independent; the throughput analysis in [[Appendix]] §D is a panel-scale projection and is marked as such. These three scales are separated by a fabrication-technology jump as well as by area, and the distinction matters: the prototype is patterned by single-field maskless UV exposure, whereas the full-spec die and panel scale require multi-field stepper lithography with sub-pixel-pitch overlay between stitched fields, or an equivalent foundry process. That transition is not specified, and nothing has been fabricated at either the full-spec or panel scale.

**Operating point.** These three scales are one axis of a larger operating point — scale (pixel count) × read length × application — on a single sensor architecture, not a family of platforms. The architecture is invariant; what moves along the continuum is where it is operated. At small array, short read, and known targets the same chip is a classification or targeted-panel device; at panel scale with mappable reads and coverage it is a genome sequencer. Read length is not a free parameter: it is gated by the interfacial sensing envelope ([[Appendix]] §E), short at the nominal operating point (~9 bases) and bounded above by convergent ceilings, so this remains a short-read architecture and no long-read regime is claimed anywhere along the continuum. Crossing from the near-term panel point to the genome point requires reads that clear the mapping floor, which is a two-component gate: long enough for unique placement (~20–25 bases, the Debye-envelope extension of [[Appendix]] §E) and accurate enough that an error-free seed survives (a lower per-cycle timing CV). Extending the sensing envelope is necessary but not sufficient — it buys length, not accuracy — and both coupled bets must land. Only the small-array short-read panel point is buildable near-term; every operating point beyond it carries its gating condition.

## B. Surface Functionalization

The surface is functionalized with a self-assembled monolayer (SAM) of thiol-anchored primers, immobilized through Au-thiolate bonds [18]. The thiol-SAM serves two roles at once: it is the primer-attachment chemistry and it is the dielectric layer whose perturbation the Au channel reads. Because the Au layer is simultaneously the DNA-anchoring surface and the electrode whose molecular-axis impedance the SAM modulates, functionalization is part of the transduction stack rather than a separate substrate-preparation step. This is a constraint the optical platforms do not share, since they separate what the DNA binds to from what generates the signal (a substrate versus a camera). Every functionalization choice below is therefore evaluated against two simultaneous requirements. It must present enough primer density and correct orientation to support bridge amplification, and it must not degrade the Au interface's own well-behaved double-layer response, which is the baseline the molecular axis measures against.

### Primer density and orientation

Three consequences follow. First, orientation control is part of what makes the ~1 µm cluster geometrically reproducible pixel-to-pixel. Second, density variance is one contributor to the ~25% pixel-to-pixel charge-transfer-resistance spread the model treats as a nuisance parameter. Third, the backfill layer, the short thiol-alcohol treatment that displaces nonspecifically adsorbed probe and sets hybridization-competent probe density [19], is itself part of the baseline dielectric environment the Au interface presents, so it is measured and held fixed by the blank-chip and post-functionalization EIS calibration steps in the validation chain; a backfill-density change is electrochemically indistinguishable from a functionalization-step change, and both are captured by the same calibration.

### Anchor chemistry and reuse

The chip's 50 to 100-run reuse estimate is bounded by how many strip-and-reload cycles the thiol-Au bond survives before thiolate desorption and oxidation degrade it [18], not by anything about the DNA itself. A conventional monothiol anchor forms a single Au-S bond per strand, and losing that one bond releases the whole strand. Multidentate anchor chemistries, a primer terminated in two or three thiol groups, chelate the gold at multiple points, so a single bond failure does not release the strand. Trithiol-capped oligonucleotides have been shown to survive thiol-competing conditions that strip monothiol-anchored strands from the same surface [20]. Whether a multidentate anchor meaningfully extends the reuse estimate at ECSEQ's operating conditions is untested and remains a proposal pending hardware validation. It is a low-risk substitution worth qualifying alongside the monothiol baseline, because surface reuse is a cost lever essentially unique to ECSEQ: every competing platform sequences on a single-use flow cell, chip, or wafer, so surface-preparation cost is a per-run cost for them and a per-reuse-cycle cost here, and the reuse multiplier is a direct multiplier on that cost advantage — developed into the platform's cost structure in [[Appendix]] §D.9.

### Between-run regeneration

Reuse requires a between-run cycle that removes the amplified DNA while leaving the thiol-Au anchor and backfill intact. This is a denaturing strip (for example a dilute NaOH or formamide wash, standard practice for regenerating thiol-DNA gold surfaces) that clears duplex DNA without approaching the potentials or temperatures that desorb thiolate. Chemical regeneration of this kind is standard practice across biosensor platforms [21], but we have not found a characterization of a SAM-preserving denaturing strip in the specific form ECSEQ requires, so the mechanism is assumed rather than established. This resets the whole surface to its pre-loading state, re-verified by the same blank-chip and post-functionalization EIS pair used at first build. The protocol details (strip reagent, exposure time, and the verification threshold for reload) are engineering parameters to be established on hardware.

### Physical confinement

Lateral isolation between pixels, the polymer insulating fill, plays the role a patterned nanowell or charged capture pad plays on competing platforms. It converts amplification from an undirected random-deposition process into one with a predefined, addressable site per cluster. High-density clonal arrays now uniformly favor geometrically predefined single-cluster sites over random deposition, because random deposition cannot suppress the co-localization failure mode (two templates merging into one unresolvable signal) at high density [4,22]. ECSEQ's version of this constraint is defined by the SAM/backfill boundary at the pixel edge rather than a physical well wall.

A second and distinct failure mode follows from that choice. Co-localization concerns two independently landed templates merging; overgrowth concerns a single, correctly seeded cluster whose own bridge amplification carries it past its allotted area. At a 2 µm pitch with a ~1 µm active site, the clearance between a well-formed cluster's edge and the neighbouring pixel's sensing zone is roughly 0.5 µm per side. That margin is set by amplification cycle count and kinetics rather than by landing statistics, and without a physical wall to arrest growth there is nothing but reaction time bounding it. No bound on cluster radius against cycle count is established here; it is a single-pixel or bulk-surface imaging measurement, independent of electrochemical readout, and it is a validation target.

## C. Preparation and Loading

Sample preparation reuses the front end of conventional short-read platforms and introduces no chemistry unique to ECSEQ. Genomic DNA is fragmented to the target read length, and adapters complementary to the two surface primers are ligated to the fragment ends so each strand can hybridize to the primer lawn. This is a borrowed, well-characterized primitive rather than a novel step. As the throughput analysis in the appendix shows, however, its wall-clock cost dominates the per-run time budget: whenever a configuration closes a genome in one run ($\eta P L \ge B$), sequencing occupies under 3% of wall-clock time — at $L = 30$ roughly 3.5 seconds against a ~35-minute prep — so $T_\text{genome} \approx T_\text{prep}$ and prep is not merely the single largest term in time-to-genome but effectively the only one, even though it is not where the platform's technical differentiation lies. Prep chemistry is not a research question for ECSEQ, but prep time is therefore the single lever on time-to-genome, and the subsections below treat fragmentation, loading, amplification, and quality control as separable levers.

### Fragmentation and Adapter Attachment

The default route — mechanical or enzymatic fragmentation followed by separate end-repair, A-tailing, and ligation — is the most universally supported and the slowest. Tagmentation collapses fragmentation and adapter attachment into one step with a hyperactive Tn5 transposase [23], and transposase-based rapid kits report hands-on times of ~ten minutes rather than hours [24]. Either would shorten ECSEQ's prep meaningfully, and neither touches the primer or adapter design at the array surface — both yield a standard adapter-bearing fragment — so the prep chemistry is a bench decision independent of the sensor. It is the most concrete, lowest-risk lever against the prep-time budget, pending a ligation-versus-tagmentation comparison against ECSEQ's specific design.

### Loading

The prepared library is flowed over the functionalized array under conditions favoring sparse, single-fragment landing at each pixel. In the language most platforms use, this is Poisson loading: the library is diluted enough that most sites capture zero or one molecule and few capture more than one, and the empty and multiply-occupied sites are discarded downstream. Unpatterned random deposition tops out well under 40% single-occupancy by Poisson statistics alone, which caps occupancy near 37% [22,25]. Competing platforms either accept this statistical ceiling or spend engineering effort past it. Some patterned flow cells race amplification kinetics against molecule arrival to suppress multi-templating at a fixed site, a mechanism specific enough to anchor its own patent family. Nanoball platforms capture pre-formed amplicons onto lithographically patterned, charged binding pads sized for one nanoball, and several bead platforms avoid on-array loading statistics altogether by amplifying clonally off-array, by emulsion PCR onto a bead, and loading only the well-amplified particles [22,26,27].

None of these routes is available to ECSEQ without cost. Off-array amplification is structurally incompatible with the sensing mechanism. The polymerase and its growing strand must sit directly at the Au/Pt electrode stack for the impedance perturbation to be observable at all, so a pre-amplified bead would still have to make the same intimate, primer-mediated contact with the functionalized electrode that direct on-surface growth already provides, at which point it adds a particle-to-electrode capture problem rather than saving a step. Kinetic-exclusion loading is a fluidics and reagent-kinetics approach not obviously incompatible with ECSEQ's chemistry, but it is a mechanism specific enough to be actively defended elsewhere in the field, and it is not the only way to solve the problem here.

ECSEQ has a loading-control option none of the surveyed platforms have, because its array is already a grid of individually addressable electrodes before sequencing begins. The same row and column addressing built for EIS readout can in principle be repurposed during loading itself. DNA is strongly polyanionic, carrying roughly one negative charge per base along the phosphate backbone, so a modest, transient positive bias applied at a target pixel during loading should locally raise the electrophoretic or dielectrophoretic capture rate of nearby template strands at that pixel relative to its unbiased neighbors, biasing single-molecule seeding toward the addressed site without racing amplification kinetics or pre-sorting particles off-array. Because the hardware this requires, per-pixel addressable electrodes with independent bias control, is already present for readout, this would be close to a zero-incremental-component loading control, unlike patterned charge deposition (a separate fabrication step) or bead sorting (a separate fluidic subsystem). Neither the achievable capture-rate bias at ECSEQ's pixel geometry and buffer ionic strength, nor its interaction with the thiol-SAM and backfill surface, has been measured yet. Active-bias loading control is a distinct mechanism from kinetic-exclusion loading and from patterned-charge-pad capture, and is gated on single-pixel hardware validation before building out further.

### Clonal Amplification

The primer lawn is also the substrate for clonal amplification. A single template does not produce enough signal to measure, so each landed fragment is copied in place by bridge amplification [28]. The free end of a surface-bound strand arches over and hybridizes to an adjacent complementary primer, a polymerase extends the resulting bridge into a double strand, and denaturation releases two surface-bound copies. Iterating this cycle grows a dense, spatially localized clonal cluster of roughly a thousand identical copies over a single pixel (Fig. 3), lifting the per-event signal from a single molecule to a measurable ensemble average. Amplification runs isothermally (Bst 2.0 WarmStart, ~65 °C), the same single operating temperature as sequencing, rather than the thermal-cycled PCR that emulsion-based platforms require for their off-array clonal step.

Bridge amplification was chosen over the alternative isothermal route, rolling-circle amplification into a DNA nanoball, for reasons beyond convention. Rolling-circle amplification requires the template to be circularized first, by a splint-ligation or protelomerase step, and produces a compact, free-floating concatemer that must then be captured onto a patterned site as a separate manufacturing step. Bridge amplification instead grows the cluster directly outward from the same primer lawn already required at the electrode surface for DNA anchoring and molecular-axis sensing, with no circularization and no separate capture event, so the cluster is never detached from the electrode. It also has the cleanest freedom-to-operate picture of the alternatives surveyed: the solid-phase bridge-amplification patent lineage has fully lapsed, while the rolling-circle nanoball route is closing but not yet entirely clear. The dated FTO analysis, with its jurisdictional and patent-term-adjustment caveats, is maintained in [[ECSEQ-0/IP & Patents/Patent Landscape & FTO Analysis]].

### Occupancy and Quality Control

Over-loaded pixels (more than one template, termed polyclonal) and under-loaded pixels (empty or failed amplification) both have to be identified and excluded before or during sequencing. Optical platforms detect polyclonal sites by imaging, since an overlapping or double-intensity cluster is visually distinguishable from a clean single cluster, at the cost of needing that image in the first place. ECSEQ obtains an equivalent diagnostic from a step the calibration chain already performs. The blank-chip and post-functionalization EIS baseline established for every pixel before sequencing sets an expected per-pixel impedance signature once a well-formed, roughly 1,000-copy cluster is present. A pixel whose post-amplification baseline falls well outside the expected single-cluster range, either too small (under-loaded or failed amplification) or anomalously shaped (multi-templated, non-clonal), is flagged the same way a blank or failed pixel is, without a separate imaging subsystem. This is a genuine advantage of electrochemical over optical readout for occupancy quality control specifically, though it has not yet been characterized against real multi-templated pixels and should be treated as an expected rather than demonstrated property of the architecture.

## D. Electrochemical Signature

Nucleotide incorporation produces localized, base-dependent perturbations in the interfacial impedance. Modeling the electrode-electrolyte interface as a series of constant-phase-element double layers coupled to a charge transfer pathway, the total impedance Z(ω) is the sum of the Pt and Au contributions, Z(ω) = Z_Pt(ω) + Z_Au(ω). Because the Au and Pt interfaces have distinct electrochemical relaxation time constants, their contributions decouple across frequency bands, encoding base identity along three physically distinct axes.

The kinetic axis dominates the lower-frequency regime (100 Hz to 10 kHz), corresponding to the Pt double-layer response [17]. During incorporation, the polymerase passes through a closed ternary conformation whose dwell time is base-dependent [11]; because the impedance perturbation persists for the duration of that state, the time course of the event modulates the impedance in this band. Individual dwell times vary stochastically [10,11], but sequence-dependent temporal patterns provide statistically significant classification features when aggregated over a cluster.

The molecular axis is captured in the high-frequency tail of the sweep (10 kHz to 100 kHz), where the Au interface response is most pronounced. This signature reflects the dielectric and dipole coupling of the incorporated nucleotide to the thiol-SAM interface, a base-dependent perturbation of order 15% in the interfacial impedance magnitude (the ~15% molecular-axis shift the readout-scaling analysis of [[Appendix]] §D.4 refers back to). The premise is grounded at both ends: the four bases differ measurably in dipole moment and polarizability [29], and DNA bound at a gold electrode measurably alters that electrode's interfacial impedance [30]. The step between them is not established. We have found no direct demonstration that per-base dipole coupling is resolvable in the 10 kHz to 100 kHz impedance band at a gold electrode, so the molecular axis as specified here is a modeling assumption to be confirmed on hardware rather than a result imported from the literature. Unlike the kinetic axis, it is dwell-independent and relies on base-intrinsic amplitude, providing orthogonal discriminatory power that separates purines from pyrimidines even where kinetic information is insufficient.

To resolve residual ambiguity within the purine (A, G) pair, we use the faradaic axis, which exploits the distinct intrinsic oxidation potentials of the natural nucleotides. Guanine has the lowest oxidation potential, followed by adenine, with pyrimidines requiring higher, more sluggish potentials [31]; the same ordering holds for direct oxidation at an electrode, where guanine and adenine oxidize by mechanistically distinct, well-separated voltammetric steps [32]. By biasing the Au electrode toward the guanine oxidation onset, we render the charge-transfer resistance base-dependent: guanine presents a characteristically minimal charge-transfer resistance, adenine a higher one. This signal appears in the low-frequency shoulder of the spectrum (20 to 90 Hz). This band assignment is a modeling assumption rather than a measured result, and it carries two caveats. Published DNA-on-gold impedance work sweeps broadly without delineating a characteristic charge-transfer band [33], and the charge-transfer time constant is system-specific, so no universal band exists to assign it to. Further, that literature almost entirely measures hybridization state through a solution redox mediator, whereas the faradaic axis proposes direct, mediator-free oxidation of the bases themselves, which is conventionally characterized by DC voltammetry rather than AC impedance [32]. Establishing that the arc is accessible in this band at ECSEQ's geometry is therefore a primary objective of single-pixel validation.

The faradaic axis introduces two hardware constraints. First, biasing the electrode at elevated potentials requires precise control to avoid degrading polymerase fidelity and template integrity. Second, the detection bandwidth is limited by polymerase kinetics; with adenine's shortest dwell time of ~66 ms, the measurable frequency floor is restricted to roughly 15 to 30 Hz, so the architecture captures only the high-frequency shoulder of the charge-transfer arc rather than the full sub-Hz plateau.

## E. Sequencing Loop

Sequencing proceeds with all four natural nucleotides co-present in a single common pool over the surface-bound clusters, which are generated on the primer lawn beforehand by isothermal bridge amplification (Bst 2.0 WarmStart, ~65 °C) [28]. A polymerase extends each surface-bound primer processively, incorporating the complementary base at each position, and that incorporation is read in real time from the impedance signature of the event. In conventional cyclic sequencing the wash exists to clear unincorporated labeled nucleotides before readout and to reset the reaction between cycles [3]. Reading the intrinsic electrochemical signal collapses that entire chain: there is no label to image, nothing to cleave, and no reagent to exchange between incorporations. Wash-free operation falls out of label-free real-time transduction rather than being engineered separately.

### Position among sequencing architectures

Most established platforms are structurally cyclic: patterned-flow-cell synthesis, nanoball synthesis, avidity sequencing, flow-based semiconductor sequencing, and expansion-based nanopore sequencing each repeat some variant of add-reagent, detect, clear-and-reset once per base or per flow, even where an individual upstream step is itself isothermal [9,26,34]. Only two surveyed architectures are truly continuous. Real-time single-molecule optical sequencing reads fluorescence from a single confined polymerase with no wash step at any point in a run [10], and native nanopore sequencing reads ionic current continuously as a strand threads a pore, though translocation sequencing is not synthesis and has no polymerase incorporation event to read [35,36]. ECSEQ is architecturally closest to the former: both are continuous, real-time, synthesis-driven, and wash-free for the full duration of a run. The difference is what each reads and at what scale. Single-molecule optical sequencing confines and observes one polymerase inside a nanophotonic structure; ECSEQ reads the electrochemical signature of an ensemble of roughly 1,000 co-synthesizing copies at one electrode. No platform surveyed combines continuous, non-cyclic synthesis with a fully electrical, label-free readout and clonal-ensemble amplification, and that combination is where ECSEQ's wash-free claim differs in kind rather than degree from the cyclic optical and electrical platforms.

### Homopolymer Runs

A repeated base, three consecutive guanines for instance, is exactly the case where the competing electrical and flow-based chemistries struggle. Flow-based semiconductor sequencing dispenses one nucleotide species at a time and infers homopolymer length from the magnitude of a single summed signal across however many bases incorporate in that flow; distinguishing a run of five from a run of six means resolving a continuous, noise-affected magnitude into an integer, and independent benchmarking identifies this failure mode as the characteristic error signature of that platform, with miscalls rising sharply as run length grows [37,38]. Raw nanopore current carries a related ambiguity for a different reason: the sensed signal depends on several bases occupying the pore constriction at once, so a homopolymer run barely perturbs the current as identical bases pass through [39,40]. At least one expansion-based nanopore chemistry exists specifically to convert each base into its own discrete coded reporter before readout, precisely so a homopolymer run produces a train of separable events rather than one smeared excursion [41].

ECSEQ's chemistry does not force this tradeoff, because it was never flow-limited. All four nucleotides are continuously present, so the polymerase never waits on a reagent-delivery cycle to advance; each incorporation, including each base within a homopolymer run, is a discrete kinetic event, a closed-fingers dwell, separated in time from its neighbors by the polymerase's own translocation kinetics rather than by an externally imposed flow boundary. Provided successive events remain resolvable at the sensor's acquisition rate, a run of identical bases should present as a train of individually dwell-resolved impedance events rather than one integrated magnitude, for the same structural reason that continuous real-time optical readout does not share the flow-based homopolymer error mode. This is an expected consequence of the architecture rather than a measured one: neither the current simulator nor the single-pixel validation plan isolates homopolymer performance, and it should be treated as a testable prediction until dwell-resolved data over a repeated-base template is in hand.

### Desynchronization without a cycle boundary

Cyclic optical platforms typically see per-base quality decay over a long read, to which phasing and prephasing are well-characterized contributors alongside signal decay and optical cross-talk [42,43,44,45]: some fraction of strands in a cluster fall behind, through incomplete extension in a cycle, or run ahead, through incomplete terminator or dye removal, at each discrete cycle boundary, and this desynchronization compounds cycle over cycle because every cluster is locked to the same shared clock. Measured per-cycle rates are on the order of 0.5 to 0.8% phasing and 0.4 to 0.6% prephasing [43]. ECSEQ has no such clock to fall behind on: each of the roughly 1,000 copies in a cluster synthesizes continuously and independently, with no wash or cleave step imposing a shared boundary all copies must clear together. That does not mean the ensemble stays perfectly synchronized. The simulator already models cluster dephasing as an independent noise source, because the individual copies' stochastic per-base kinetics naturally spread their incorporation events apart over the length of a read even without a cycle boundary to fail at. The mechanism differs in kind from cycle-driven phasing, being a gradual per-molecule kinetic drift rather than a compounding failure at a shared reset point, but the practical consequence is the same shape of problem: read length is ultimately bounded by how far the ensemble's copies drift apart before their summed signal stops resolving cleanly into one incorporation per position.

It is important not to read the absence of a cycle boundary as an advantage here. Removing the shared clock removes the compounding failure at a reset point and pays for it with an unbounded random walk, and on the incorporation kinetics assumed throughout this paper the exchange is unfavorable. The appendix evaluates it (see [[Appendix]], §E): the per-cycle timing coefficient of variation is 0.485, so the ensemble's positional spread grows as $0.485\sqrt{n}$ bases and crosses one full base within about four cycles. That is a per-cycle positional drift roughly two orders of magnitude larger than the 0.5 to 0.8% phasing a cyclic optical platform contends with, not smaller. The wash-free architecture buys continuous operation and a simple instrument, and the price it pays is a short read.

### Reagent Supply Over the Run

Because there is no per-cycle wash, nucleotide consumption and reaction byproducts, pyrophosphate and protons, accumulate continuously rather than being cleared and reset each base. Continuous real-time optical sequencing runs a single loaded reaction chamber to completion with no reagent exchange during the run, an option available to it because the observed volume per confinement site is extremely small and the total DNA synthesized across a run is correspondingly tiny [10]. ECSEQ's ensemble clusters consume substantially more nucleotide per pixel than a single molecule would, over a run intended to reach several hundred bases across roughly 1,000 copies per cluster and up to the full pixel array. Whether the bulk reagent pool needs periodic replenishment over a run, a slow run-scale top-up categorically different from a per-base wash-and-exchange, or can run from a single loaded volume, is an open engineering parameter rather than a settled design choice. It does not by itself reintroduce a wash step: a bulk exchange on the timescale of an entire run, minutes to tens of minutes, is a different operation with different fluidic requirements than the per-base reagent exchange a cyclic platform performs hundreds of times over the same run.

### Faradaic Interrogation

The faradaic axis runs by default, and the loop admits one additional scheduling decision because of it: whether the elevated-potential guanine-oxidation interrogation runs continuously alongside the benign kinetic and molecular sweep, or as a short pulse interleaved between longer benign-potential periods to bound the polymerase's cumulative exposure to elevated potential. A pulsed schedule trades a small amount of faradaic-axis duty cycle, and therefore some fraction of the events that benefit from the third discrimination axis, for a bounded, quantifiable exposure budget per run rather than an unbounded continuous one. Which schedule is necessary, and what duty cycle is safe, is among the questions single-pixel hardware validation is designed to answer; it is a sequencing-loop-level operational choice, with the underlying physics covered in the electrochemical-signature section.

# 5. Model

The base-classification model has two stages: a per-cycle encoder that compresses each spectrum into an embedding vector, and a temporal transformer that reads the whole cluster's embedding sequence and predicts each base in context (Fig. 4). The two-stage split mirrors the structure of the problem. A single incorporation event is ambiguous, so the encoder's job is not to call the base but to organize a clean, separable representation of it; the temporal stage then resolves the ambiguity by reading each event alongside its neighbors.

![[whitepaper-encoder-transformer-architecture.png]]

## A. Input Representation

Each pixel reports one complex impedance spectrum per incorporation cycle — a current simplification we flag here rather than leave implicit: the model as evaluated operates on *pre-segmented* events, whereas real acquisition is a free-running frame stream at fixed cadence, with no incorporation trigger and events at arbitrary phase. Detecting where an incorporation occurs is properly an output of the basecaller, not an input to it, and inferring that segmentation is deferred work (see §7). The spectrum is a 62-point frequency sweep composed of a 50-point main band (100 Hz to 100 kHz) and a 12-point faradaic sub-band (~20 to 90 Hz). The model does not consume the raw spectrum but the change between two states on the same pixel, the impedance during an incorporation minus that pixel's quiescent baseline, expressed in polar form as a log-magnitude change and a phase change at each frequency and concatenated into a 124-dimensional input vector (Fig. 5, Fig. 6).

$$
\Delta\log|Z| = \log\!\left(|Z|_{\text{event}} / |Z|_{\text{baseline}}\right), \qquad \Delta\varphi = \varphi_{\text{event}} - \varphi_{\text{baseline}}
$$

This representation is chosen for two physical reasons. First, working in the log-magnitude difference makes the input a ratio against the pixel's own baseline, which cancels per-pixel offset. Absolute impedance varies substantially across the array (charge-transfer resistance spans roughly 25% pixel-to-pixel, approximately log-normal, driven by fabrication and functionalization tolerances), but the within-pixel event-versus-baseline ratio is stable regardless, so the encoder never has to learn around a per-pixel scale it cannot see at inference. Second, phase is orthogonal to magnitude: the phase change captures the shift in reactive/resistive balance that magnitude alone misses, and together they form the natural polar description of an EIS perturbation.

Dwell time is carried alongside the spectrum as a separate scalar rather than folded into it, because dwell is a distinct physical quantity, the polymerase's base-dependent closed-fingers residence time [11], not a spectral feature. It is log-normalized against a 100 ms reference so the network sees a signed deviation from typical dwell rather than an absolute count.

## B. Encoding Stage

The first stage is a tri-branch 1D convolutional network whose three branches mirror the three discrimination axes. A kinetic branch convolves over the low-frequency band (roughly 100 Hz to 10 kHz) carrying the Pt dwell-correlated signal; a molecular branch spans the 100 Hz to 100 kHz main sweep, carrying the Au dielectric/dipole signal; and a faradaic branch reads the ~20 to 90 Hz sub-band, carrying the base-dependent charge-transfer signal that separates A from G. Making the frequency decomposition explicit gives each branch a well-scoped target rather than forcing one network to disentangle the axes from the full spectrum. The molecular branch is deliberately given the full 100 Hz to 100 kHz main sweep rather than only the 10–100 kHz tail where the Au response physically dominates (§4D), so it can reference the dielectric perturbation against the lower-band baseline instead of assuming the tail's location a priori; its overlap with the kinetic band is a modeling choice, not an inconsistency with the physical axis assignment. The kinetic and molecular branches are convolution stacks (channel widths 64, 128, 256, with GELU activations, batch normalization, and light dropout), the faradaic branch a lighter stack (32, 64, 128), each followed by global average pooling. The three pooled outputs are concatenated (640-dimensional) and projected to a 256-dimensional embedding, onto which the log-normalized dwell scalar is concatenated and projected back to 256 dimensions.

The encoder is trained as a per-event classifier that reaches ~50% per-event accuracy on the current L=30 reads, far short of production grade because a single event remains ambiguous; its purpose is to structure a separable embedding space. It carries three heads during pre-training: a primary 4-class head (A/C/G/T), a purine-versus-pyrimidine auxiliary head, and a G-versus-not-G auxiliary head that gives the faradaic branch a direct gradient toward the guanine-oxidation feature the other heads are blind to. The purine/pyrimidine objective organizes the embedding along the coarse molecular axis the physics separates most cleanly, so the temporal stage inherits a representation in which the within-group pairs (A/G, C/T) are already partially separated; label smoothing softens the two confusable pairs during training. The faradaic branch supplies the per-event separation of the A/G purine pair the other two axes lack; on measured reads this axis does its job, and guanine in particular is resolved cleanly (see §6). A quantified faradaic-on versus faradaic-off ablation at the current read length is not yet available and is deferred to a dedicated run.

## C. Temporal Stage

The second stage is a transformer encoder over the sequence of per-cycle embeddings. Its context window is an architectural capacity of ~500 cycles, which should not be read as a claim about physical read length — [[Appendix]] §E bounds the latter to tens of bases, and the window is sized well above it so that the same model serves any read length the sensing geometry turns out to permit. Over that window, with sinusoidal positional encoding and pre-layer-norm blocks (on the order of 6 layers, 8 attention heads). Because basecalling is offline, attention is bidirectional, so each position conditions on both upstream and downstream context. The raw per-cycle dwell is injected a second time here, as a learned per-position bias, because the encoder's fused embedding discards most fine-grained dwell statistics and the sequence model benefits from seeing them directly. A linear head maps each position to four base logits, one set per pre-segmented event. On a real free-running stream this stage becomes a segmenting decoder over frames rather than a per-position classifier over events — a change that strengthens the two-stage split rather than weakening it, since the encoder is already a frame featurizer and the temporal stage already integrates context. This stage carries most of the remaining accuracy, resolving ambiguity single events cannot by reading each base in the context of its neighbors' kinetics and local sequence, lifting the call from the encoder's ~50% per-event to 71.2% per-base (Phred Q5.4) on the current L=30 reads — a substantial gain, but still short of the Q20 (99%) production floor, with the residual and the path to closing it detailed in §6. The stage emits calibrated per-base posteriors, not bare argmax calls: every downstream variant caller consumes per-base quality and weights by it, so a calibrated softmax distribution over {A,C,G,T} is a required output of the architecture rather than an optional add-on.

## D. Training

The model is currently trained on synthetic reads from a physics-based simulator that produces ground-truth-labeled spectra by modeling the full chain from polymerase kinetics through Pt/Au transduction, cluster dephasing, and correlated noise. Training proceeds in staged phases rather than end-to-end from scratch, which stabilizes the two differently-shaped stages and keeps cost down. The encoder is pre-trained first on individual events under the multi-task objective above. Its weights are then frozen and its embeddings pre-computed once over the full dataset, so the temporal transformer trains against fixed embeddings without re-running the encoder each step. A short lower-learning-rate phase fine-tunes the temporal stage, and an optional final phase unfreezes the encoder for end-to-end fine-tuning at a much smaller encoder learning rate, protecting the pre-trained features while letting the two stages co-adapt. Training runs in bfloat16 under AdamW throughout.

Because the model must emit usable quality scores, calibration is a training-time objective alongside accuracy, evaluated by expected calibration error (ECE) and a reliability diagram rather than by accuracy alone. On the current reads the raw softmax is already well calibrated (ECE 0.045), and temperature scaling does not improve it, so the raw posteriors are shipped directly as per-base quality scores.

Post-fabrication, this synthetic-first procedure inverts: the simulator is recalibrated against the measured parameters of the real chip, and the model is fine-tuned from its existing checkpoint onto real data rather than retrained from scratch. A learned frequency-band selector, a differentiable mask that collapses the 62-point sweep to only the frequencies carrying signal [46,47], both speeds hardware acquisition and doubles as a separability diagnostic; it is a proposed refinement gated on hardware validation and is developed in the appendix (see [[Appendix]], §D).

# 6. Experiments

Every result below is measured on simulated data, not on hardware. The simulator generates ground-truth-labeled spectra by modeling the full chain from polymerase kinetics through Pt/Au transduction, cluster dephasing, and correlated noise, so the numbers report the decoder's ability to invert that simulated physics, not a measurement of a device. They are pre-hardware checkpoints on the array's signal, and characterize two things: the accuracy the two-stage decoder reaches, and the structure of its residual error, which locates where the model fails against where the signal model predicts it should.

**Simulator provenance.** The simulator's parameters are literature-derived where a source exists — polymerase dwell distributions and base ordering [10,11], base dielectric and oxidation properties [29,31,32], and the CPE-Randles interface model ([[Appendix]] §A) — and estimated where none does: the cluster-dephasing rate, the molecular- and faradaic-axis band assignments, and the correlated-noise magnitudes. The estimated parameters are exactly the assumptions enumerated in the §4.F register, and their count — not any single value — is the honest measure of how far these results rest on the simulator rather than on measured physics. Each is retired at the post-fabrication recalibration step (§5D), when the simulator is fit to the measured chip before the model is fine-tuned.

We report two training runs on the same architecture at two read lengths. The **short-read run** ($L=30$) is the regime the sensing geometry permits ([[Appendix]] §E) — reads of tens of bases, the operating point of targeted panels and resequencing. The **long-read run** ($L=100$) sits beyond that horizon and so measures decoder capacity given long context rather than a supported operating point; we report it because it bounds what the temporal stage can do and isolates the faradaic axis's contribution, and then state why the physics excludes it. Each is a small section below.

## A. Short-read run ($L=30$)

We evaluate the full pipeline on held-out simulated reads with the faradaic axis enabled; accuracy is reported per-event (the encoder calling each incorporation in isolation) and per-base (the temporal stage calling each position in cluster context), both four-class problems at 25% chance. The per-event encoder reaches 50.1%, the expected shortfall of a single ambiguous spectrum before temporal context. A paired faradaic-on/off ablation has not been run at this read length — that comparison is reported for the $L=100$ run in §6.B — but the faradaic axis is visibly doing its job in the per-class results below, where the guanine and A/G channels are essentially resolved. The temporal stage carries the call the rest of the way, lifting per-base accuracy to 71.2%, Phred Q5.4 under $Q = -10\log_{10}(1-\text{acc})$; unfreezing the encoder for a final phase changes this negligibly (71.0% to 71.2%), confirming the frozen-encoder representation is already near the joint optimum (Fig. 7). This is well short of the Q20 (99%) production floor and the Q30 (99.9%) that consensus over coverage would target.

Four caveats bound these numbers. Every figure is a single-call accuracy (per-event or per-base), not a consensus over reads; consensus is not yet computed and, because the residual is correlated, is not expected to follow the naive binomial that would otherwise lift it well above the per-base figure — a correlation-aware error model is left for hardware validation. The accuracy is measured against simulated ground truth, so it is a statement about the model and simulator together, not a chip; its value is in showing the three-axis signal carries enough information to discriminate all four bases and in locating the residual where the physics predicts. The simulator implements the attrition dephasing bound of [[Appendix]] §E.1 rather than the phase spread of §E.2, so the ensemble is in phase by construction and these numbers do not yet test calling a base from a smeared ensemble in which off-position copies contribute inter-symbol interference. And every figure assumes perfect event segmentation — one spectrum per incorporation — which hardware does not provide, so the reported residual is a substitution residual only, with insertion/deletion modes unrepresented.

## B. Long-read run ($L=100$), and why the physics excludes it as an operating point

At a longer read the same architecture becomes a different measurement. A read of $L=100$ exceeds the array's Debye sensing horizon ([[Appendix]] §E) and is not a supported operating point; what it isolates instead is decoder capacity when temporal context is abundant, and the marginal value of the faradaic axis. On $L=100$ simulated reads the temporal stage reaches 89.6% per-base, and the per-event encoder rises from 54.5% with the two physical branches (kinetic and molecular) to 76.7% once the faradaic branch is added — the third axis converting the unresolved A↔G purine pair into a callable one. Read as a statement about the array, this says the simulated signal carries enough information for a decoder to approach 90% given long context; read as a product claim it says nothing, because the context it needs is longer than the read the physics allows. These numbers predate the short-read run and share its four caveats; they are reported for what they bound, not as an operating point.

Why the physics excludes that operating point is worth stating directly. The complementary regime — a single read of hundreds of bases, the province of nanopore and single-molecule platforms — is the natural target for *de novo* assembly and repeat spanning. The signal ECSEQ reads is the impedance perturbation of an incorporation event, observable only while the polymerase active site sits inside the electrode's Debye screening envelope — roughly three Debye lengths, about 3 nm, or some nine bases, at the ionic strength an active polymerase requires ([[Appendix]] §E). Each base synthesized carries the active site 0.34 nm farther from the electrode, so the event falls out of the sensing volume within tens of bases regardless of how long the polymerase would otherwise process. This is a property of interfacial electrostatics, not of the model or the readout, and no amount of compute lifts it. The only route to a long read on this platform is a different transduction mechanism — sensing a diffusive byproduct of incorporation, as semiconductor sequencing does — which decouples read length from electrode distance but forfeits the per-event molecular specificity that lets ECSEQ keep all four bases co-present and drop the wash (§7). The long read is therefore outside the design space of this instrument, and the platform's reach beyond sequencing (§1, §7) does not depend on it, because the continuous-monitoring and surface-binding applications sit at the electrode surface, where the screening limit that bounds sequencing does not apply.

**Data and code availability.** The physics simulator and dataset generator, the two-stage basecaller, and the end-to-end readout feasibility study are maintained in the project monorepo (`data/`, `dodgson/`, and `READOUT/` respectively); the release terms under which they will be made available are to be determined.

# 7. Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work

We have characterized ECSEQ, a passive EIS electrode array read out by a learned decoder, and developed it against its hardest application, label-free and wash-free sequencing by synthesis. Two claims organize the result. The architectural claim is that reading incorporation electrically rather than optically moves the throughput bottleneck off the fluidics and onto readout electronics and compute, so the figure of merit is an architecture-independent areal base rate (the $\eta P / t_\text{base}$ of the abstract and [[Appendix]] §D), under which a single-run genome is a parallelism condition rather than a time-to-result one, contingent on an array scale not yet built. The physical claim is that the incorporation signal separates into three frequency-distinct axes carrying enough information to discriminate all four bases, with a faradaic axis introduced to break the purine pair. Both are claims about the array and its signal; the decoder and the application are the parts meant to change.

Several limitations bound these claims. First, and most important, all results are on simulated data: the accuracy figures reflect the fidelity of the physics simulator, not a measurement on hardware, and the transition to a real chip is a calibration-and-validation step whose outcome is not yet known. Second, the faradaic axis, which the model relies on for A↔G separation, is the least established of the three. The assignment of its signal to the 20 to 90 Hz band and the compatibility of elevated Au bias with polymerase fidelity are both modeling assumptions to be tested; and beyond either, the axis is destructive by mechanism, since guanine oxidation is irreversible and the measurement chemically consumes the base it reads. That imposes a cumulative-exposure budget over a run and a marginal-signal dilution against previously oxidized bases still inside the sensing envelope, neither of which the reported accuracies model. Third, and now the binding constraint on read length, the incorporation signal is observable only inside the electrode's Debye sensing envelope — tens of bases, derived in §6.B and [[Appendix]] §E — so ECSEQ should be understood as a short-read architecture until a measured per-copy cycle-time distribution says otherwise. Dephasing is a real second ceiling converging at the same order but is not the primary one; the open question it raises is not whether the cluster stays in phase, because it does not, but whether the basecaller can deconvolve a continuously smeared ensemble — nanopore basecalling in kind, with a known model class to attack it. Fourth, the surface-reuse estimate that underpins ECSEQ's cost argument is a mechanism, not a measurement, and the multidentate-anchor chemistry proposed to extend it is untested at operating conditions. Fifth, the basecaller's residual error is correlated rather than independent across reads: the characteristic failure is the C/T pyrimidine pair and thymine in particular, and because it arises from a fixed missing discrimination axis rather than from random noise, it repeats at the same reference position across independent reads and cannot be assumed to average out with coverage. Partial mitigations exist and are partial by nature — calibrated per-base posteriors consumed by a caller that models the confusion structure, both-strand observation, and, decisively for the pyrimidine pair, a fourth discrimination axis or stronger dielectric contrast that the faradaic axis does not provide by design.

The short read this implies has a consequence for how ECSEQ's output must be used, and it is a bioinformatics-quality argument rather than a time-to-genome one. Reads of tens of bases place the platform in the reference-based resequencing regime, alongside established short-read sequencing at 2×150 bp, which is fully viable for human genomes and for the targeted-panel and diagnostic applications the platform is aimed at. *De novo* assembly and the spanning of long repeats are out of scope for this architecture unless the sensing mechanism itself changes, and we do not claim them. Separating this from the throughput analysis matters, because a single read-length figure otherwise silently carries two unrelated claims — how fast a genome can be produced, and what can be resolved once it is.

## The read-length, accuracy, and parallelism triangle

Read length, per-base accuracy, and array parallelism are not three separate topics but one constraint, and stating it as one is what bounds the feasible region rather than leaving it open. Three inequalities must hold together for a one-run genome:

- **Throughput** — enough usable bases per run: $\eta P L \ge B$, derived in [[Appendix]] §D. A short read is compensable by pixel count here, and only here.
- **Usability** — a read is only useful if it maps: $P(\text{read error-free}) = \text{acc}^L$, so accuracy enters as a hard gate, not a quality dial (see §6).
- **Physics** — read length is itself bounded: $L \le \min(L_\text{geom}, L_\text{coh})$, with both terms landing at tens of bases ([[Appendix]] §E).

The three bind simultaneously. The physics inequality forces short $L$; the usability inequality then converts per-base accuracy from a quality metric into a viability gate, because $\text{acc}^L$ collapses fast at small acc and small $L$ (at 71.2% and $L=30$, $P \approx 3.7\times10^{-5}$, ~8.6 errors per read); the throughput inequality is the one short reads can still satisfy, by trading $L$ against array area rather than against time. The short-read platform is therefore not free: it is viable only once per-base accuracy reaches the mapping gate at the achievable read length. Showing that this feasible region exists and was bounded — rather than assumed away — is the point of stating the triangle as one constraint.

Stated at the platform level (and laid out as operating points in §4), ECSEQ is not one product but one architecture read at different points on a continuum: the near-term targeted-panel device and the eventual whole-genome sequencer are the same sensor, not a platform and its successor, and addressable market expands up that ladder as the physics matures and the array grows. Every rung above the targeted panel is gated — the gate for whole-genome being the two-component mapping floor, length and accuracy, not read length alone — and every rung stays short-read: whole-genome is reached by mappable short reads plus coverage, as established short-read sequencing already does at 2×150 bp, not by long reads.

Beyond sequencing, the same passive array and learned decoder define a broader instrument, and its most distinctive applications are the ones sequencing's read-length limit does not touch. The Debye screening envelope bounds sequencing because synthesis carries the event away from the electrode; a measurement of something bound at the surface — a hybridized target, an antibody-antigen pair, a redox-active metabolite — sits inside that envelope permanently and is not read-length-limited at all. Because the readout is continuous and the throughput bottleneck is compute rather than fluidics, such measurements can run as long-duration, real-time monitoring rather than one-shot assays, and each inherits the same compute-scaling advantage as the sequencer. Label-free pathogen detection, targeted molecular panels, and continuous multi-analyte biomarker monitoring are the natural next chips on the same reader; we name them as directions the architecture supports rather than as results, each carrying its own validation from first principles. What they share with sequencing is the entire stack beneath the application — the array, the reader, the decoder, and the simulator-and-calibration flywheel each new chip inherits from the last.

## The next experiment, pre-registered

The whole program gates on one measurement: does a single natural-base incorporation, in a clonal cluster on a ~1 µm² Au/Pt pixel, produce a reproducible, base-discriminating impedance signature above the noise floor? [[Risks & Kill-Criteria]] converts that into pre-registered pass/fail gates, summarized here. The numeric thresholds are set from the blank-chip baseline *before* the run, not after — a threshold chosen after seeing the data is not a kill-criterion — and per-run cost and duration are engineering estimates maintained in that document.

| Risk (ranked) | Single-pixel gate | If it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cluster dephasing caps read length | phase coherence usable to ≥ a pre-set cycle count on a known template (KILL-1) | controlled-cycle, phase-tracking, or single-molecule fallback |
| Per-event SNR at 1 µm² | per-event ΔZ/Z mean clears measured noise σ by ≥ a pre-set SNR (KILL-2) | sensor / active-area redesign before scale-up |
| Molecular (Au dielectric) axis under-grounded | resolvable per-base dielectric contrast in the 10–100 kHz band | fourth discrimination axis or stronger contrast |
| Sweep-vs-kinetics timing | acquisition fits inside the ~66 ms incorporation window | pulsed / sub-banded acquisition schedule |
| Per-base accuracy insufficient to map | reaches Q20 at the achievable read length (§6) | not viable for resequencing without further model or axis gains |

The immediate future work is therefore hardware: fabricating a single pixel, running the blank-chip and post-functionalization calibration, recalibrating the simulator against the measured interface, and fine-tuning the basecaller onto real reads. Beyond that, the throughput and read-length analysis in the appendix defines the readout back end required to reach whole-genome scale, and the learned frequency-band selector offers a route to shortening the acquisition window once the informative bands are confirmed on a real device. One acquisition idealization remains and is the natural next basecaller-side step: event segmentation. The reported accuracies assume incorporation events are already delimited, but the device delivers an unsegmented stream, so removing that assumption means calling bases from a continuous signal with the alignment inferred rather than given — a CTC or transducer problem, the same class that carried nanopore basecalling from ~65% to >99% on an unchanged physical signal. If the per-event signal survives the move to hardware at the fidelity the simulator predicts, the near-term instrument is a short-read, application-agnostic electrochemical sensor — targeted DNA panels first, and the surface-binding and continuous-monitoring assays above on the same reader with a retrained decoder. The sequencing ladder above that is gated in a definite order: first per-base accuracy to Q20, the mappability threshold everything downstream rests on (§6); then array area (~2,200 mm² at 0.5 µm pitch), the fabrication variable that decides whether a genome closes in one run; then the multiplexed readout back end, feasible in an end-to-end simulation of the readout chain (Appendix §D.8) and now gated on a single front-end-noise measurement rather than open in principle; and last sample preparation, which dominates wall-clock time but is borrowed and already met with margin. At the top of that ladder is the aspiration that motivates the platform rather than a claim it makes today: massively parallel, low-cost sequencing at population scale — the substrate a general genotype-to-phenotype model, mapping sequence to physical expression, would ultimately require. The short-read instrument characterized here is the first step toward that array.
