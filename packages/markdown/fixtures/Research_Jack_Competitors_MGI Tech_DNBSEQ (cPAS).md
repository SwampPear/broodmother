# DNBSEQ (cPAS) — MGI Tech / Complete Genomics

Status: **currently selling** (multiple instrument generations shipping worldwide). See [[#Regulatory & geopolitical positioning]] for material US-market caveats. Researched/compiled 2026-07-17; regulatory section updated 2026-07-17 with confirmed BIOSECURE Act enactment details.

MGI Tech Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen) is majority-owned by BGI Group and is the hardware arm that spun out of the original Complete Genomics acquisition. **Complete Genomics Inc.** (San Jose, CA) is MGI's US-facing commercial entity, selling the same underlying instruments (T7, T20×2, G99, etc.) under its own brand. Both brands are covered together here because they are the same technology, not a different one — see [[#Regulatory & geopolitical positioning]] for why the brand split exists.

## Pipeline: hair strand → prepared library → DNA nanoball → electronic/optical readout

1. Extract and purify genomic DNA from the hair root bulb (cell lysis, protein/RNA digestion, column or bead purification) → double-stranded gDNA in buffer.
2. Fragment gDNA (enzymatic or acoustic shearing) to a few-hundred-bp insert size and ligate MGI adapters carrying sample indexes and known primer-binding regions — same conceptual step as Illumina library prep, but the *downstream* amplification chemistry is completely different from here on. See [[Sequencing by Synthesis (SBS)]] for contrast.
3. Denature the adapted, double-stranded library to single strands.
4. A **splint oligo** anneals to both ends of one single strand, holding them adjacent; DNA ligase seals the nick, circularizing the molecule into a single-stranded DNA circle. This circularization step is the branch point that makes DNBSEQ's amplification physically different from bridge PCR or emulsion PCR — there is no exponential PCR step at all past this point.
5. Exonuclease digests any DNA that failed to circularize (linear leftover), then the reaction is cleaned up, leaving only closed ssDNA circles.
6. A rolling-circle-amplification (RCA) primer anneals to an adapter sequence on each circle.
7. Phi29 DNA polymerase (strand-displacing, non-exponential) extends the primer around the circle repeatedly. Because Phi29 displaces rather than copies-and-releases, one circle produces **one long, continuous, single-stranded concatemer** — hundreds of tandem copies of the same insert covalently joined in a single molecule. This is linear (isothermal) amplification, not PCR: it avoids PCR's exponential duplication-bias and chimera-formation failure modes (source: MGI technology white paper; independently discussed in Patterson et al., 2021 — GigaScience, PMC7953489).
8. The long ssDNA concatemer self-coils under its own base-pairing into a compact ball roughly 200 nm in diameter, containing on the order of 300–500 tandem copies of the original fragment — the **DNA nanoball (DNB)**.
9. RCA is halted with a stop buffer once nanoballs reach target size (over-long RCA produces DNBs too large to seat correctly in an array well).
10. DNBs are loaded onto a **patterned array** — a silicon or glass surface pre-etched with a regular grid of positively-charged spots sized and spaced to bind almost exactly one DNB apiece (reported pitch ~600–900 nm center-to-center on current-generation arrays; source: Kim et al., *Sci. Rep.* 2024, "Deep learning enables the use of ultra-high-density array in DNBSEQ," Nature, 2024-11 — peer-reviewed, high confidence for the pitch figure, though exact pitch varies by product generation). Because the array is patterned (not random, unlike Illumina's older non-patterned flow cells), DNB positions are geometrically constrained, which suppresses the "co-localization" cluster-overlap failure mode that limits loading density on unpatterned surfaces.
11. Sequencing primers hybridize to the repeated adapter sequence present in every copy within a DNB.
12. **Sequencing-by-synthesis** proceeds cycle by cycle: polymerase + fluorescently labeled reversible-terminator nucleotides (standard chemistry), OR — on CoolMPS-chemistry instruments — polymerase + *unlabeled* nucleotides plus four base-specific fluorescent antibodies that bind the just-incorporated base non-covalently (see [[#CoolMPS chemistry variant]]).
13. Each cycle: incorporate one base/probe → wash → excite four (or two, in dichromatic “2 + 2” schemes on some low-cost instruments) fluorescence channels → camera images the whole array → software calls the base from each DNB's spot color → cleave dye/terminator (or wash off antibody) → repeat. Because every copy within a DNB is a template for identical, in-phase synthesis, the DNB behaves like a single very-bright emitter — functionally the same "amplify-then-optically-image" logic as an Illumina cluster, but built by RCA instead of bridge PCR.
14. After the programmed cycle count (e.g., PE100, PE150), reads are demultiplexed by index, then aligned to a reference genome for variant calling, exactly as with any other short-read platform.

## Platform profile

- **Chemistry**: combinatorial Probe-Anchor Synthesis (**cPAS**) — despite the "combinatorial probe-anchor" name inherited from the technology's 2010s ligation-based ancestor, current cPAS is a true polymerase-driven sequencing-by-synthesis chemistry, not ligation. (Historical note, discontinued: the *original* Complete Genomics platform sold ~2010–2013, before BGI's 2013 acquisition, used **cPAL** — combinatorial Probe-Anchor *Ligation* — a genuinely different, non-polymerase chemistry. It has not been sold since Complete Genomics Inc. was reconstituted under BGI/MGI ownership; current instruments do not use it. Status: **discontinued**, publicly reported, high confidence.)
- **Detection**: 4-channel (or 2-channel low-cost variants) fluorescence imaging, same optical principle family as Illumina SBS.
- **Amplification**: rolling-circle amplification (isothermal, linear, Phi29 polymerase) producing DNA nanoballs — no PCR/bridge-amplification step. This is the single physically distinguishing feature vs. Illumina.
- **Array architecture**: patterned silicon/glass array with regularly spaced charged binding sites sized for one DNB each; delivered as sealed flow cells (G-series, T7) or, on the highest-throughput T20×2, as open silicon-wafer "slides" cut from a full wafer rather than a cartridge flow cell (publicly reported, MGI/GenomeWeb AGBT 2023 coverage).
- **Typical applications**: WGS (population-scale and clinical), WES, RNA-seq, single-cell (via partner kits, e.g. PIPseq), NIPT (MGI/BGI's original commercial anchor application), oncology panels, agrigenomics.
- **Major advantages**: no exponential PCR amplification bias/chimera formation; patterned array with reported <1% duplication rates; CoolMPS option removes base-linked dye "scars" that otherwise accumulate across cycles; independently and company-reported low mismatch/indel rates in recent benchmarks (see [[#Application specialization and physical basis]]); consumable cost per Gb has historically undercut Illumina, driving MGI/Complete Genomics' "sub-$100 genome" marketing claims.
- **Major limitations**: DNB size (~200 nm) sets a practical floor on spot pitch, so at the very highest densities, ball-to-ball crowding becomes a basecalling/optical-crosstalk problem — addressed partly via the deep-learning basecaller described above; no long-read product (DNBSEQ is short-read only, max reads to PE300/SE400, so it does not address SV/phasing/repeat-region problems that long-read platforms do); global commercial availability is constrained by US trade-policy exposure (see next section).
- **Regulatory/clinical positioning**: strong footprint in China (NMPA-cleared instruments, e.g., DNBSeq-T1+, DNBSEQ-E25 medical-device registration valid 2025-01-10 to 2030-01-09 — publicly reported, GenomeWeb/Yicai Global) and CE-IVD marking in Europe/Asia-Pacific for T7, G400, G50 (publicly reported, GenomeWeb). No FDA-cleared MGI/Complete Genomics sequencing instrument was identified in this research pass (search performed 2026-07-17) — labeled **unavailable/not found**, medium confidence this reflects reality rather than a search gap, given the additional US regulatory friction below.

### Regulatory & geopolitical positioning

This materially shapes MGI/Complete Genomics' addressable market and must be read alongside the technology, not as a footnote:

- The 2024 draft of the US **BIOSECURE Act** explicitly named BGI, MGI, and Complete Genomics as entities of concern; that version did not pass as part of the FY2024 NDAA (publicly reported, Foley Hoag/Ropes & Gray legal alerts, 2025–2026).
- A revised BIOSECURE approach was folded into the FY2026 NDAA and, per late-2025/early-2026 legal reporting, enacted; the newer bill defines "biotechnology company of concern" (BCC) status procedurally rather than by naming companies outright. As of the most recent source found (Ropes & Gray, dated 2026-01), the DoD's "1260H list" includes **BGI Group (which includes MGI Tech Co., Ltd.)** but — notably — **not** Complete Genomics Inc., WuXi AppTec, or WuXi Biologics. **Confidence: medium** — this is a fast-moving legal/legislative area, sourced to law-firm client alerts rather than primary statutory text, and the list is subject to revision. Verify current 1260H list status before relying on this for any procurement decision.
- Practical effect: Complete Genomics Inc. (the US-branded, formally separate legal entity) currently appears able to sell/support instruments in the US federal-adjacent market in a way MGI Tech Co., Ltd. directly may not — this is the commercial logic behind maintaining two brand names for identical hardware.

### CoolMPS chemistry variant

CoolMPS is a chemistry option (not a different physical pipeline) available on PCR-free DNBSEQ instruments. Instead of fluorescently labeled nucleotides, it uses **unlabeled** natural nucleotides plus four monoclonal antibodies (one per base) that bind the incorporated base and carry the fluorescent label non-covalently. Because no dye/linker is left attached to the DNA backbone after cleavage, CoolMPS avoids the cumulative chemical "scarring" that labeled-nucleotide chemistries leave behind cycle after cycle.

- Independently published, peer-reviewed: Drmanac et al., *Nucleic Acids Research* 2021 (PMC7826284) — non-coding RNA sequencing evaluation of CoolMPS.
- Company-reported: ~3-fold signal-intensity improvement and elimination of scar interference (MGI technical communications, 2020); up to 55% reduction in WGS indel errors on PCR-free DNBSEQ vs. PCR-cluster-based sequencers (MGI, sourced to a Google-collaboration study referenced in MGI's own blog — **label: publicly reported by vendor, not yet independently corroborated in this research pass; confidence low-medium** pending an independent replication).

## Application specialization and physical basis

- **Short-read WGS/WES/panels**: strong fit — this is the platform's core design point, directly analogous to Illumina's use case.
- **Amplification-bias-sensitive applications** (e.g., quantitative applications like ChIP-seq, low-input RNA-seq): isothermal RCA/DNB formation is linear, not exponential, so it does not compound GC-bias or over-representation the way PCR-based cluster generation can — a genuine physical advantage, though the *library-prep* PCR step upstream (adapter completion) still exists and can itself introduce bias regardless of the sequencing chemistry.
- **Homopolymer/indel accuracy**: peer-reviewed and vendor-sourced benchmarks (GigaScience 2021; MGI/Google-referenced 2025-2026 comparison) report DNBSEQ platforms performing at or above NovaSeq 6000/Element AVITI/PacBio Onso on SNP and indel F-measure in a NIST GIAB HG002 benchmark, with a reported 30× mismatch rate of 0.34% vs. >0.5% for NovaSeq 6000 and PacBio Onso in the same comparison. **This specific multi-platform figure traces back to MGI's own published blog post citing a Google collaboration; treat as company-reported until an independently authored paper is located — confidence low-medium.** The earlier, fully independent GigaScience 2021 study (7-platform Korean Reference Genome comparison) found MGI platforms had higher SNP-chip concordance than older Illumina HiSeq2000/HiSeq4000 systems specifically, not necessarily current NovaSeq X — confidence high for what it actually measured, but it predates NovaSeq X and AVITI, so it is not a current-generation comparison.
- **No long-read/SV/phasing/repeat-region capability**: DNBSEQ is exclusively short-read (max ~PE300/SE400); it is not competitive for structural-variant detection, haplotype phasing, or de novo assembly of repetitive regions — those require the long-read platforms covered elsewhere in this vault (see comparisons note).
- **Direct RNA / native modification detection**: not supported — cPAS sequences amplified DNA copies, not native single molecules, so it cannot detect base modifications directly (unlike nanopore).

## Standardized scalability analysis

**Horizontal scaling** (adding sensing sites): MGI's stated roadmap for going from G99 → G400 → T7 → T7+ → T20×2 is explicitly horizontal — same cPAS chemistry, same per-DNB physics, more flow cells/slides run in parallel and higher DNB density per unit area. The T20×2's move to open silicon-wafer slides (vs. sealed cartridge flow cells) is itself a further horizontal step: more sensing area per run without changing the underlying chemistry.

- DNB pitch (peer-reviewed, Kim et al. 2024): ~600–900 nm center-to-center on current high-density arrays. Using a representative 700 nm pitch: theoretical site density ≈ 1 / (700×10⁻⁹ m)² ≈ 2.0×10¹² sites/m² ≈ ~2.0 million sites/mm². **Calculated from reported data**; this is a theoretical maximum geometric site density, not an occupancy-adjusted usable-site count — no independent source in this pass reported total physical site count or occupancy % for any specific flow cell, so "active sites" and "occupancy rate" for DNBSEQ are labeled **unavailable** here. As a proxy, vendor-reported "reads per flow cell" (e.g., 5,000M for T7, 12,000M for T7+) already represents post-filter, passing-read counts by standard NGS-industry convention, so these numbers approximate *usable* sequencing sites more directly than a raw geometric-density calculation would.
- Instruments needed for 1 Tb/day: T7 (≈6–7 Tb/day at max config) → **<1 instrument** needed nominally; T7+ (>14 Tb/day) → well under 1 instrument. **Calculated from reported data**, assumes full 4-flow-cell configuration run continuously, which is an optimistic (theoretical-max) utilization assumption, not typical realistic throughput.
- Instruments to reach 100 / 1,000 / 10,000 human genomes/year (30× ≈ 90–100 Gb/genome, assume ~90% realistic uptime vs. vendor's implied 100%):
  - T7+ vendor-claimed capacity: 35,000 genomes/year at full utilization → ≈31,500/year at 90% realistic uptime. **1 instrument comfortably covers 100, 1,000, and 10,000 genomes/year** at this throughput class — calculated from reported data, high sensitivity to the uptime assumption, confidence medium.
  - T20×2 vendor-claimed capacity: 50,000 genomes/year → ≈45,000/year at 90% uptime; same conclusion, one instrument covers all three tiers. Confidence medium (vendor-claimed input figure).

**Vertical scaling** (making each site/instrument faster): MGI has driven vertical gains chemistry-side — CoolMPS (better signal, fewer scarring artifacts, not literally faster), and the deep-learning basecaller enabling *higher density on the same physical footprint* (Sci. Rep. 2024) is arguably a vertical improvement expressed as horizontal capacity (same instrument footprint, more sites resolved correctly). Cycle time reductions between G99 (Q40 PE150 in ~12h) vs. G400 (PE150 in ~37h) reflect a genuinely faster chemistry/imaging cycle on the newer, lower-throughput instrument, illustrating that MGI's "fast/benchtop" line (G99) and "high-throughput" line (T7/T7+/T20×2) trade off cycle speed against parallel site count differently — not a single vertical-scaling story across the whole product line.

- Bases/active site/second: not independently calculable from public data (cycle time and per-cycle base count are reported, but total active-site count per flow cell is not) — **unavailable**.
- Primary physical bottleneck: DNB packing density vs. optical/basecalling crosstalk at very short pitch — this is the ceiling the deep-learning basecaller work is explicitly trying to push back (Sci. Rep. 2024 title: "Deep learning enables the use of ultra-high-density array"), which is itself evidence that crosstalk-at-density is the binding constraint MGI engineers are currently fighting. Confidence: medium (inferred from what the R&D literature is solving for, not a direct vendor statement of "this is our bottleneck").

## Competitive positioning

DNBSEQ instruments compete directly, workflow-for-workflow, with Illumina's short-read line at matched throughput tiers: DNBSEQ-G99 vs. Illumina MiSeq i100/NextSeq 1000; DNBSEQ-G400 vs. NextSeq 2000/NovaSeq 6000 (lower config); DNBSEQ-T7/T7+/T20×2 vs. NovaSeq X/X Plus — same customer (core sequencing labs, population genomics programs, clinical labs), same budget line, same short-read WGS/WES/panel workloads, same lab infrastructure (standard library-prep kits, standard bioinformatics pipelines with FASTQ/BAM/VCF outputs). The switching barrier is less about chemistry and more about (a) the BIOSECURE/1260H exposure discussed above suppressing US public-sector and some pharma adoption regardless of technical merit, and (b) Illumina's larger installed base of validated clinical assays and software ecosystem (BaseSpace, DRAGEN) that many labs are contractually or operationally locked into. Full cross-company competitive-landscape tables are consolidated in the comparisons note (see [[Instrument & Market Comparison]]).
